                  Laboratory Rules, Safety & Equipment Handling Guidelines
      To ensure safe operation of laboratory equipment and effective learning, all students must strictly
      follow the rules given below.

      1. Preparation Before the Lab
          • Come prepared with the digital logic circuit diagrams, IC pin details, Truthtable for the
             assigned experiment.
          • Read the experiment objective, theory, and procedure in advance.
          • Bring the lab record, data sheets, and required stationery.
          • Follow the experiment sequence and lab schedule as prescribed by the department.
          • Ensure that you have the required software tools or IDE familiarity before starting the
             experiment (when you are using simulators / FPGA kits).

      2. General Laboratory Rules
          • Enter the laboratory on time and with proper preparation.
          • Use computers, digital trainer kits, FPGA kits only for academic purposes.
          • Follow instructions given by the faculty or lab instructor at all times.
          • Work only on the system and hardware kits assigned to you.
          • Maintain discipline, silence, and cooperation with other students.
          • Do not move around unnecessarily during the lab session.
          • Keep your workstation clean and organized.

                                                                                                         5
      Department of Computer Science & Engineering, RVCE3. Equipment Handling Guidelines
    • Handle digital trainer kits, FPGA kits and related cables/modules carefully.
    • Use proper cables / patch cards / jumper wires and connectors while making circuit
       connections.
    • Avoid applying excess force on pins, connectors, and ports.
    • Ensure all components are properly connected before switching ON the power supply.
    • Return all components and tools to their designated storage location after use.
    • Report damaged components or malfunctioning equipment immediately to the lab
       instructor.

4. Electrical & Hardware Safety
    • Do not power the circuit until the instructor verifies the connections.
    • Avoid short circuits, reversed polarity, and incorrect power supply connections.
    • Always switch OFF the power supply before modifying the circuit.
    • Do not touch exposed wires or powered circuits unnecessarily.
    • Ensure proper ground connections when interfacing sensors or modules.
    • Use only the recommended voltage levels for conducting the experiments

5. Computer & Network Usage Policy
    • Do not install, uninstall, or modify software on laboratory computers.
    • Do not tamper with system files, network settings, or servers.
    • Do not use another student’s login credentials.
    • Save program files only in the designated folder.
    • Do not connect external storage devices (USB drives, CDs, etc.) without permission.
6. Prohibited Activities
Students are strictly prohibited from:
    • Coming late to the laboratory or leaving early without permission.
    • Changing the assigned system or hardware kit without informing the lab instructor.
    • Removing any hardware component from the laboratory.
    • Using laboratory equipment for non-academic activities.
    • Completing the lab record instead of performing the experiment during lab time.

7. End of the Lab Session
Before leaving the laboratory:
    • Save your program files and shut down the computer properly.
    • Disconnect circuits carefully and switch OFF the power supply.
    • Return all components and tools to the designated place.
    • Ensure the workspace is clean and organized.
    • Place the chairs back in their original position.

Note:
Failure to follow laboratory rules and safety instructions may result in denial of lab access or
disciplinary action.

Theory:

The availability of large variety of codes for the same discrete elements of information results in the
use of different codes by different systems. A conversion circuit must be inserted between the two
systems if each uses different codes for same information. Thus, code converter is a circuit that
makes the two systems compatible even though each uses different binary code.

Parallel adders are digital circuits that compute the addition of variable binary strings of
equivalent or different size in parallel. The IC 7483 is 4 bit binary adder, adds two 4bit binary
numbers. numbers being processed are A4 A3 A2 A1and B4 B3 B2 B1, while the answer is S4 S3
S2 S1. Adder can be used as subtractor, by feeding 2s compliment of 2nd number (B). This is done
by producing 1s compliment (using inverters- exor gates are used to selectively invert the input) and
then adding logic 1 (by feeding logic 1 at the carry input). The circuit shown below, acts as adder /
subtractor, based on the control input fed to one of the inputs of EX-OR gate. If control input is
logic 0, circuit acts as adder, if control input is logic 1, circuit acts as subtractor.

Note: When used as subtractor, always remember, the carry produced is opposite of what is
expected. (compliment of the actual carry).

Department of Computer Science & Engineering, RVCEBinary Coded Decimal:

Binary Coded Decimal is a method of using binary digits to represent the decimal digits 0 through
9. It is possible to assign weights to the binary bits according to their positions. The weights in the
BCD code are 8, 4, 2 and 1. Ex: (137)10 - BCD equivalent (0001 0011 0111)2.

Excess-3 Code:
This is an un-weighted code. Its code assignment is obtained from the corresponding value of BCD
after the addition of (0011)2.

BCD to Excess-3 (or) Excess-3 to BCD: Since each code uses four bits to represent a decimal
digit, there must be four inputs and four output variables. The input variable are designated as B3,
B2, B1, B0 and the output variables are designated as E3 , E2, E1, E0 in the truth table.

Theory
IC-74153 is a dual 4-to-1 multiplexer have 2 sets of 4:1 MUX name MUX A and MUX B,
and the IC has 2 select lines S0 and S1 for each of the MUX but these selection line are
internally shorted together as shown in the logic diagram below and hence the entire IC has
only two selection pins. Each 4:1 MUX is associated with an active low enable pin namely
Ea and Eb. With appropriated logic level on enable pins and on selection pins, logic levels
on the input pin of any one or both the MUX can be transferred to the output. Za and Zb are
the output of MUX A and MUX B respectively.

Theory:
Flip-Flop
A flip-flop circuit can maintain a binary state indefinitely (as long as power is delivered to
the circuit) until directed by an input signal to switch states. A flip-flop has two useful states,
when Q=1 and Q ’ = 0, it is in the set state (or 1-state). When Q = 0 and Q’ = 1, it is in the
clear state (or 0-state). The outputs Q and Q’ are complements of each other and are referred
to as the normal and complemented outputs, respectively. The binary state of the flip-flop
is taken to be the value of the normal output.

The NAND basic flip-flop circuit operates with both inputs normally at 1 unless the state of
the flip-flop has to be changed. The application of a momentary 0 to the set input causes
output Q to go to 1 and Q ’ to go to 0, thus putting the flip-flop into the set state. After the set
input returns to 1, a momentary 0 to the reset input causes a transition to the clear state. When
both inputs go to 0, both outputs go to 1 – a condition avoided in normal flip-flop operation.

JK Flip-Flop
A JK flip-flop is a refinement of the RS flip-lop in that the indeterminate state of the RS type
is defined in the JK type. Inputs J and K behave like inputs S and R to set and clear the flip-flop
(note that in a JK flip-flop, the letter J is for set and the letter K is for clear). When inputs are
applied to both J and K simultaneously, the flip-flop switches to its complement state, that is,
if Q=1, it switches to Q=0, and vice versa.

Master – Slave Flip-flop:
A master – slave flip-flop is constructed from two separate flip-flops. One circuit serves as a
master and the other as a slave, and the overall circuit is referred to as a master-slave flip
-flop. It consists of a master flip-flop and a slave flip-flop. When clock pulse CP is 0, the
output of the inverter is 1. Since the clock input of the slave is 1, the flip-flop is enabled and
output Q is equal to Y, while Q’ is equal to Y’. The master flip flop is disabled because CP =
0. When the pulse becomes 1, the information then at the external R and S inputs is
transmitted to the master flip-flop. The slave flip-flop, however, is isolated as long as the
pulse is at its 1 level, because the output of the inverter is 0. When the pulse returns to 0, the
                                                                                                    33
Department of Computer Science & Engineering, RVCEmaster flip-flop is isolated, which prevents the external inputs from affecting it. The slave
flip-flop then goes to the same state as the master flip-flop.

IC74192:
74HC192: 74HCT192 is a 4-bit synchronous decade binary up/down counter.
Separate up/down clocks, ClkU and ClkD respectively, simplify operation. The outputs
change state synchronously with the LOW-to-HIGH transition of either clock input. If the CLKU
clock is pulsed while CLKD is held HIGH, the device will count up. If the CLKD clock is
pulsed while CLKU is held HIGH, the device will count down.

Only one clock input can be held HIGH at any time to guarantee predictable behavior. The
device can be cleared at any time by the asynchronous master reset input (Clr); it may also be
loaded in parallel by activating the asynchronous parallel load input.

The terminal count up (Carry) and terminal countdown (Borrow) outputs are normally HIGH.
When the circuit has reached the maximum count state of 09, the next HIGH-to-LOW transition
of CLKU will cause carry to go LOW. Carry will stay LOW until CLKU goes HIGH again,
duplicating the count up clock. Likewise, the Borrow output will go LOW when the circuit is
in the zero state and the CLKD goes LOW.

The terminal count outputs can be used as the clock input signals to the next higher order circuit
in a multistage counter, since they duplicate the clock waveforms. Multistage counters will not
be fully synchronous, since there is a slight delay time difference added for each stage that is
added.

The counter may be preset by the asynchronous parallel load capability of the circuit.
Information present on the parallel data inputs (P0 to P3) is loaded into the counter and
appears on the outputs (Q0 to Q3) regardless of the conditions of the clock inputs when the
parallel load input is LOW.

 Department of Computer Science & Engineering, RVCE                                        36A HIGH level on the master reset input will disable the parallel load gates, override both
clock inputs and set all outputs (Q0 to Q3) LOW. If one of the clock inputs is LOW during and
after a reset or load operation, the next LOW-to-HIGH transition of that clock will be
interpreted as a legitimate signal and will be counted. Inputs include clamp diodes. This
enables the use of current limiting resistors to interface inputs to voltages in excess of VCC.

a. Ring counter : A ring counter is a type of counter composed of a type circular shift
register. The output of the last shift register is fed to the input of the first register. Ring
counters are used in hardware logic design (e.g. ASIC & FPGA) to create simple finite state
machines. A binary counter will require an adder circuit which is substantially more complex
than a ring counter. Additionally, the worst-case propagation delay on an adder circuit will
be proportional to the number of bits in the code (due to the carry propagation).
The propagation delay of a ring counter will be a constant regardless of the number of bits
in the code. The complex combinational logic of an adder can create timing errors which
may result in erratic hardware performance. Also, ring counters with Hamming distance 2(or
more) allow the detection of single bit upsets that can occur in hazardous environments.

The disadvantage of ring counters is that they are lower density codes. A binary counter can
represent 2^N states, where N is the number of bits in the code, whereas an Overbank counter
can represent only N states and a Johnson counter can represent only 2N states. This may
be an important consideration in hardware implementations where registers are more
expensive than combinational logic.
